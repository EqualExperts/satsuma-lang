# The Satsuma Diaries
## Friday, 31 July 2026

> **tl;dr** — The tool learned to tell you *how much of your work you've actually finished*. Type `satsuma coverage` and it counts how many of your fields have been mapped and how many are still sitting there untouched. Then, within about two hours of shipping it, the contributors found three separate bugs in how it counted and fixed all of them.

So we had a week of basically nothing and then all of it arrived at once.

Quick word on the nothing first, for the record. Between the 23rd and the 30th the only thing that happened was the dependency butler turning up with four more parcels — a test tool, some type definitions, a YAML reader — and someone adding a temporary folder to the ignore list so it stops getting swept up into commits. That's it. Eight days. One of the parcels sat unopened until Friday morning. Peak.

**Friday morning was the washing-up.** Eight of those parcels went in one after another, and then the same tiny library from last time — **brace-expansion**, the thing whose entire job is turning `file{1,2,3}` into three filenames — came back with a *third* warning against it, and this one said every version of the old line was affected with no fix coming. So the contributors did the sneaky-but-legitimate thing: they went round the two projects still dragging that old copy in and forced a newer version of the library above it in the chain, after checking that neither of them used the one feature the newer version dropped. Then they pinned the Python code-checker to an exact version, because it had quietly updated itself under CI and started enforcing a whole new rulebook nobody had opted into — that's 53 complaints to work through, all fixed, one of them argued with rather than obeyed. And then a thing I do think is genuinely good: the Excel-to-Satsuma converter's tests had been broken for a while and *nobody knew*, because they weren't run automatically. They're now run on every change. A test nobody runs is a decoration, bruv.

**Then, after lunch, the actual thing.** `satsuma coverage`. Right, so: the whole point of this project is describing where data comes from and where it goes — field by field. A big mapping spec has hundreds of these. Until today, nothing told you *how far through you were*. You could look at a file and have absolutely no idea whether it was finished or half-abandoned. Now you run one command and it tells you: this source schema, 9 of 13 fields consumed; this target, 15 of 21 filled in; here's a list of the ones nobody touched. And you can wire it into your automated checks so a change that drops below, say, 80% fails the build — which turns "we should finish that mapping" from a nagging feeling into a rule.

The decision baked into it that I think is the interesting one: it only counts **leaves**. If your data's got shape to it — a `customer` containing an `address` containing three lines — the `customer` and `address` bits aren't counted, only the actual values at the tips. Because a container isn't a thing you map, it's a box you put mapped things in, and counting boxes lets you print a lovely percentage that means nothing. They wrote that down as a formal decision record so nobody re-argues it in four months.

**And then it started falling over, which is the bit I enjoyed.** Because as soon as you build a number, people go looking at the number, and the number was wrong three different ways.

First — and this one's a beauty — coverage was matching fields by **name** rather than by **where they live**. So if you had a `name` at the top of your schema and another `name` tucked inside an address, mapping either one credited both. Silently. Upward, always upward. Fixing that alone tanked a bunch of files to zero, because a second bug was cancelling it out: when a mapping pulls from several sources at once, you write `crm_customers.email -> email`, and the counter never worked out which schema `crm_customers` was. So the two bugs had been holding hands, one inflating and one deflating, and had to be fixed together. Four example files moved afterwards — three of them *down*, one of them up. Coverage figures going down is the sound of a number becoming honest.

Second, the plain-English notes. Satsuma lets you write a mapping as a sentence — `"take @first_name and @surname"` — with those `@` bits pointing at real fields. Two bugs in there: one where a nested field's location got thrown away and the reference got stapled to the top of the schema instead, and one where the resolver *found* the right nested field and then reported only its short name, dropping the path. The tell was gorgeous, actually — the tool would emit both a correct trail and a wrong one for the very same line of code. Nothing beats a program contradicting itself as evidence. And on the back of that they reversed a decision from that same afternoon: those `@` references *do* count towards coverage now, because resolving `@surname` to a declared field is a structural lookup, not the tool pretending to understand English.

Third, quietly worst: the parser's test suite — 315 checks that the language's grammar still parses everything it's meant to — had a path in it that could pass having run absolutely nothing. Fixed, and the reason it silently no-op'd is now written down in the instructions.

Somewhere in the middle of all that, three new feature plans got written and a fourth one — hierarchical coverage, i.e. "make the counting properly understand nested shapes" — got a spec and its own pile of tickets.

Thirty-nine commits, seven merged requests, three formal decision records, one new command. On a Friday. The contributors did not go outside.
